## Executive Summary

### Title
**Social Media as an Unofficial Advisory System: Trust and Reliance on Crowdsourced Academic Advice Among AAU Undergraduate Students**

### Institutional Affiliation
- **Institution:** Addis Ababa University
- **College:** College of Natural and Computational Sciences
- **Department:** Department of Information Systems
- **Course:** Research and Technical Writing for Information Systems (INSY 3061)
- **Advisor:** Dr. Ermias Abebe

### Research Problem
While informal peer advice shared via social media (notably Telegram groups) is immediate, free, and accessible, it operates in the complete absence of institutional oversight or verification. Students making critical academic choices - such as selecting courses, preferring instructors, or ranking departments during freshman placement - face considerable risks when acting upon biased, outdated, or inaccurate peer claims. Currently, empirical evidence is lacking regarding how Ethiopian undergraduates assess the trustworthiness of distinct peer sources (seniors, classmates/friends, and anonymous posters) and how such trust drives actual reliance on informal guidance.

### Research Questions (RQs)
- **RQ1:** Which advice givers (seniors, friends, or anonymous posters) do students trust most, and is this trust related to how much they rely on their academic advice on social media?
- **RQ2:** Which academic decisions are influenced by this advice, and what problems do students face after following it?

### Research Objectives
- **General Objective:** Investigate how trust in various peer source types influences undergraduate students' reliance on crowdsourced social media advice and examine the resulting impacts on their academic decision-making at Addis Ababa University.
- **Specific Objective 1:** Measure students' trust across seniors, friends, and anonymous posters and determine how far each predicts reliance on informal academic advice (RQ1).
- **Specific Objective 2:** Describe how crowdsourced advice influences course registration, instructor choice, and freshman department stream allocation, and identify the problems students encounter after acting on it (RQ2).

### Theoretical Framework
The study bridges two complementary models:
1. **Multidimensional Trust Model (Mayer, Davis, & Schoorman, 1995):** Evaluating trust along three core dimensions:
 - *Perceived Ability (Competence)*: Does the source have direct expertise and experience?
 - *Perceived Benevolence*: Does the source genuinely care about helping fellow students?
 - *Perceived Integrity*: Does the source provide honest, unbiased, and objective advice?
2. **Information Adoption Model (IAM - Sussman & Siegal, 2003) & Cognitive Heuristics (Metzger & Flanagin, 2013):** Evaluating how argument quality and source credibility influence information adoption in digital environments.

### Methodological Overview
- **Design:** Quantitative, cross-sectional survey design with supplementary open-ended qualitative incident prompts.
- **Target Population:** Addis Ababa University undergraduate students participating in peer Telegram channels.
- **Sampling Strategy:** Convenience sampling across active Telegram channels with soft quotas balancing academic year (Freshman, 2nd, 3rd, 4th Year) and discipline/department.
- **Target Sample Size:** $N = 150 \text{--} 200$ valid responses.
- **Primary Analysis Plan:** Descriptive statistics, scale reliability (Cronbach's $\alpha$), within-student ANOVA comparing trust across sources, multiple linear regression predicting reliance, chi-square tests for decision impacts, and thematic categorization of reported problems.
